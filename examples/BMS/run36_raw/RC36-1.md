## 2. THE TAIL ANSWER (asked first, as registered)

### 2.1 The shape beyond P90

The class does not have a lognormal tail. It has a fat tail, and the mechanism changes at roughly P90.

Three regimes, and the boundary between the second and third is the substantive finding:

**Below P50 — hard floor, near-symmetric.** The class is bounded below by something real: a hosted enterprise system with several actor populations, an approval workflow, third-party integrations and enterprise non-functional obligations cannot be delivered for materially less than the team-months it takes to stand up environments, agree interfaces and pass a client UAT. Lucky runs compress this modestly. They do not collapse it. The left side of the distribution is the tame side.

**P50 to P90 — multiplicative accumulation, lognormal-like.** This is the ordinary bad run: rework, requirement clarification that arrives late, integration counterparties who answer slowly, a UAT that finds more than expected. Each event multiplies rather than adds, which is why the region is lognormal in shape. Nothing structural has changed; the project is still recognisably the project that was estimated.

**Beyond P90 — regime change, power law, no natural ceiling.** The events that populate the tail are not "more of the same". They are events after which *the project is no longer the project that was estimated*: an ambiguous requirement is re-read in the expensive direction; the integration counterparty turns out not to have the API that was assumed; the platform choice is reversed after a load or security review; the client reorganises and the sponsor changes; the vendor is replaced and the work restarts. These are self-reinforcing (each one buys time in which another can occur) and they have no upper bound imposed by the original scope. That is the generating process that produces a power law rather than the tail of a lognormal, and it is the single most robust finding in the IT-project overrun literature.

The best-supported quantification of that shape is Flyvbjerg, Budzier et al. (*Journal of Management Information Systems*, 2022, n = 5,392 IT projects), which fits a power law to the upper tail of cost overrun with an exponent of roughly α ≈ 1.2 — below 2, meaning the **variance is infinite** and the **mean is dominated by the tail**. I hold the *shape* claim at high confidence and the *exponent number* at medium confidence. Its practical consequence is blunt: in this class an average overrun figure is not a forecast of anything, and a symmetric error bar around a point estimate is a category error.

A second finding from the same body of work, which I hold at medium confidence and which matters here: **in IT, cost overrun does not decline with project size.** Unlike infrastructure, a small IT project is not protected by being small. I therefore do not thin the tail on the grounds that this is a seven-person build.

### 2.2 Right-censoring — why any class-derived P99 is an underestimate

The datasets that produce these tails are drawn from projects that **finished**. Roughly 15-20% of projects in this general population are cancelled or restarted (Standish CHAOS; low confidence in the number, medium-high in the existence of the phenomenon). Cancellation is not random with respect to overrun — it removes the worst runs from the record. So the observed effort distribution is systematically *thinner* than the true one, and the censoring bites hardest exactly in the region the registered question asks about.

This forces two readings of P99, which I report separately because collapsing them is what makes tail numbers useless.

### 2.4 The class scenario at each tail point

**P95 — 4,600 PD (≈ 2.7 × P50).** *One axis of the estimate changes regime.* The canonical class run: a declared narrowing is overturned by the client after the contract is signed. In this class member the assumption log has already identified where such an overturn would land — A4 (one or two aggregators versus direct integration with many hotel systems), A8 (technical resilience versus business mass-rebooking during a disruption event), A9 (a support console versus a support product). I am not pricing any of those; the class statement is that **projects estimated at RFP stage from a document that reads two ways in more than one place have, at a rate of roughly one in twenty, at least one of those readings resolved against the estimator, and the resolution arrives too late to renegotiate cleanly.** Concurrently and typically: a 19,000-person enterprise's security, architecture and data-protection review cycles impose iterations the supplier did not foresee because it had no client counterpart at bid time. Duration at this point is roughly 2.5-3 years elapsed, or the same calendar with a team grown to 11-14 FTE and the coordination penalty that implies.

**P99 within-regime — 8,500 PD (≈ 5 × P50).** *Several axes change regime, and the contract prevents exit.* The class picture is specific and recognisable: what was scoped as a nine-to-twelve-month first release is, three years later, still called the same project. Full team turnover has occurred at least once, so the knowledge built in year one is not present in year three. At least one core subsystem has been rebuilt rather than extended, because the original shape could not carry the requirement as it was finally read. The client's own organisation has changed — new travel policy, new procurement rules, a new sponsor who did not commission this and asks why it is not finished. The supplier continues because withdrawing costs more in relationship and reputation than continuing. This is the region Flyvbjerg's "one in six projects overruns by around 200%" (HBR 2011, n = 1,471) describes; that finding places roughly 17% of projects at or above a doubling, which is consistent with my P80-P90 band, and the 1% point sits far above it.

**P99 unconditional — regime change including termination.** Here the honest answer is that **effort ceases to be the right unit**. The class scenario is: the project is stopped somewhere between P80 and P95 of spend — 2,500 to 4,500 person-days already consumed — with no production system, and is either abandoned or re-let to a different supplier who starts again and spends a further amount drawn from the same distribution. The total across attempts lands in the 5,000-10,000 PD region, but the delivered value of the first attempt is near zero, which no effort quantile can express. **A P99 stated as a single effort number implicitly assumes the project completes, and that assumption is doing more work than the number is.** I state 8,500 PD as the within-regime figure and refuse to present it as unconditional.

**On the naive extrapolation.** If one takes the class's fitted tail exponent literally and extrapolates from P90, the ratio P99/P90 is 10^(1/α) — about 6.8 at α = 1.2, about 4.6 at α = 1.5. Applied to P90 = 3,400 PD that yields 15,000-23,000 PD. I report this because suppressing it would misrepresent the class, and I decline to endorse it because the region of the dataset that supports α ≈ 1.2 is populated by multi-vendor programmes and public-sector platforms, not by single-vendor seven-person builds. My position is that the exponent is right about the *shape* and wrong about the *reachable magnitude* for this class member — and that the mechanism which stops it reaching 20,000 PD is precisely termination, i.e. the unconditional reading above, not any thinning of the underlying process.

### 2.5 What the tail answer depends on that I cannot see

The single largest determinant of where this project actually lands beyond P90 is **whether anyone intervenes** — de-scopes, converts to time-and-materials, splits the release, or declares the first release done at 60% of scope. That is blind spot 6 below, it is invisible to the outside view by construction, and it is the reason a class-derived tail should be read as *what happens if nobody acts*, not as a prediction. Everything above assumes no intervention.

## 3. UNIT DISCLOSURE

### 3.1 What one person-day is in my figures

**One person-day = one recorded, assigned working day of one person charged to this project.** Nominally eight hours. It **contains** that day's stand-up, planning and refinement meetings, code review given and received, mail and chat, context switching, and internal project coordination. It is **not** eight hours of task work. Under this convention, the focused task content of one of my person-days is realistically **4.5 to 6 hours** — call it 5. Anyone converting my numbers into "ideal engineering hours" must divide by roughly 0.6, and anyone converting ideal hours up to my unit must divide their sum by roughly 0.6 as well.

I state this deliberately because it is the most common silent divergence between an outside-view sensor and a decomposition sensor: bottom-up estimates are habitually produced in ideal task time and then reported in the same word, "day". A 1.6× gap can be manufactured by units alone.

### 3.2 Leave, holidays and sickness

**Outside the figures.** Annual leave, public holidays and sick days are not charged to the project and are not in my person-day counts. The consequence is a conversion rule that must not be skipped:

- Calendar year ≈ 261 weekdays.
- Less ~11 public holidays → ~250.
- Less ~22 days annual leave → ~228.
- Less ~5 days sickness → **~220-225 available charged days per person-year.**

So converting my effort quantiles to elapsed calendar for a fixed team uses **220 days per person-year, not 250**. Using 250 shortens every duration in this report by about 12%, and that error compounds with team size.

### 3.3 Which roles are inside

Inside: every role named in A3 — PM/BA, architect, developers, QA, DevOps — for the scope defined in A1 (design, development, testing, production deployment of the first release, including environment set-up and UAT support, and the hand-over residue A1 lists).

Outside: the client's own effort (EPAM staff time in workshops, UAT, approvals); pre-sales and bid effort; and the continuing hosted service, support function and periodic upgrades, which A1 correctly carries unpriced for want of a term.

### 3.4 What convention my sources use, and where they disagree

This is where the honest answer is uncomfortable: **my sources are not consistent with each other, and the inconsistency is large.**

| Source used | Its native convention | Leave in or out | Conversion I applied |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISBSG Development & Enhancement Repository | Recorded hours, stratified by "resource level"; the majority of records are **Level 1 = development team only**, excluding project management, client-side effort and often infrastructure roles | Out (only charged time is recorded) | **Uplifted by 25-35%** to reach an all-roles figure. This is the largest single conversion in this report and it is an estimate, not a lookup. |
| COCOMO II | Person-month defined as **152 hours**, explicitly net of weekends, holidays and vacation; covers requirements through integration test; **includes** project management effort | Out, explicitly | Converted at 152 h = 19 PD. No role uplift needed. |
| Vendor / agency build-cost quotations | Billable hours; PM sometimes inside, sometimes quoted as a 10-15% add-on; DevOps and hosting frequently outside | Out | Treated as all-roles where PM was stated inside; uplifted where it was not. Additionally discounted as *bids rather than actuals* — see §5. |
| Flyvbjerg (HBR 2011; JMIS 2022), Standish CHAOS, Jørgensen | **Ratios of cost to estimated cost.** Unit-free. | N/A | None needed. These are my relative anchors and they convert cleanly into any unit, which is exactly why they are the more trustworthy half of my evidence. |

**Size of the inconsistency.** An ISBSG Level 1 record and a services vendor's full-team billable figure for the same delivered system differ by the share of PM, BA, architecture and DevOps — typically **20-35% of the full-team figure**, i.e. a factor of roughly **1.25-1.5**. That is not noise; it is comparable to the whole distance from my P50 to my P80. If another sensor in this pipeline quotes an ISBSG-derived productivity rate and I quote an all-roles figure, **we will differ by about 1.3× for unit reasons alone and neither of us will be wrong.** The diagnostician should check the unit before attributing any divergence to method.

A second, smaller inconsistency: COCOMO II's 152-hour person-month assumes ~19 charged days per month, i.e. ~228 per year, which agrees with my 220-225. ISBSG and vendor quotes are silent on annualisation because they report totals, not rates. So the leave question is settled consistently across sources; the *role scope* question is not.

## 4. THE REFERENCE CLASS

### 4.1 Definition

**Bespoke multi-portal enterprise web systems, built for a single corporate client by an external software services supplier, delivered as a supplier-hosted service, priced at RFP stage before any discovery, first production release, small-to-mid delivery team (5-10 FTE), workflow-and-integration-centred, low transaction volume.**

The stage qualifier is not decoration. Systematic estimation error in this class is a function of stage before it is a function of anything else, and **RFP / pre-discovery / competitive** is the stage with the largest and most reliably negative bias: no client counterpart has answered a question, no API document has been read, and the estimate is produced in an environment that rewards a lower number.

### 4.2 Membership criteria — stated as categories of work, not as size

A project is in this class if it contains all eight of the following branches. I list them structurally because a project that carries whole branches this one lacks, or lacks branches this one carries, belongs elsewhere regardless of how similar the headline size looks.

1. **Several distinct user-facing surfaces for different actor populations**, with different privilege models and different mental models — not one application with role flags.
2. **A state-machine / approval workflow core** with notifications, which the client expects to be able to change later.
3. **Integrations with systems the supplier does not control**, including at least one third-party commercial API and at least one client-internal API whose documentation the supplier has not seen at bid time.
4. **A configuration and administration surface** whose purpose is to let the client change business rules without a code release.
5. **Reporting** as a named client obligation.
6. **Enterprise non-functional obligations**: federated sign-on, transport security and data-protection compliance, high availability and disaster recovery, environment and release management across dev/stage/prod.
7. **The supplier hosts and operates**, so environment build-out and hand-over artefacts fall inside the delivery project rather than outside it.
8. **Estimated from a written document with no discovery phase**, no access to the counterparty systems' documentation, and no named client respondent.

### 4.3 Why this project is in it

It exhibits all eight. Three portals plus an internal support surface (1); a five-stage approval lifecycle with email and SMS notification and an explicit demand that the process be extensible (2); CTC, UPSA, SSO, an aggregator-class booking API and Uber (3); an Administration Portal whose stated purpose is configuration and rules (4); reporting named as a functional requirement (5); the whole NFR table (6); "SaaS-based … which the Supplier will then host and support" (7); and A11's ten unasked questions (8).

The transaction volume — 5,000 hotel rooms and 1,000 taxis a year, i.e. roughly twenty transactions a working day — is a **class-membership fact, not a sizing fact**. It is what keeps this project out of the high-volume neighbour below. It does not shrink the build, because every branch above must exist regardless of how many rows pass through it.

### 4.4 Which neighbouring classes this is *not* in, and which way each would move the answer

| Neighbour | What it carries that this lacks, or lacks that this carries | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Package / COTS implementation** (Concur, TravelPerk, SAP Travel configured) | Replaces the entire "build three portals and a workflow core" branch with configuration, data migration and organisational change management; adds licence cost and vendor dependency | **Down** on build effort, substantially; up on licence and change cost |
| **Departmental internal tool** (one portal, one integration, hosted by the client's own IT) | Lacks the external-supplier-facing branch, the multi-actor branch, and the hosting/hand-over branch | **Down**, roughly by half |

| Class | Confidence | If it is really this, the answer moves |
|---|---|---|
| **Stated class** (bespoke multi-portal enterprise web system, services vendor, RFP stage, 5-10 FTE) | **60%** | — |

Misclassification therefore contributes its own right-skew, on top of the class's internal skew. This is a reason my upper quantiles are wide, and it is a reason not to narrow them.

## 6. SYNTHESIS

**Do the independent anchors agree? Three of four do; the fourth disagrees in a way that is informative.**

Anchors A1 (1,250-2,000), A2 (800-2,350 after uplift) and A3 (1,000-2,500) overlap substantially and centre in the region **1,400-1,700 PD**. That is a genuine cross-check: A1 is derived from team shape and cadence, A2 from a functional-size productivity repository, A3 from market pricing. They share no derivation and they land together. Within this method, agreement between anchors constructed from different evidence is the only validation available, and it is present here.

A4 (COCOMO II, 3,400-6,800) disagrees, sitting 2-4× above the other three. I discount its number heavily for dating — it was calibrated on a development world that no longer exists, and its over-prediction for framework-based web systems is a known and documented failure mode. But I do not discard it. Its message, stripped of its number, is that **the upper body of this distribution is a real and populated region, not estimator paranoia.** It supports the P80-P90 band; it does not move the median.

**The bid-versus-actual correction.** Anchor A3 is bid-derived and anchor R6 says bids in this class run 20-40% below delivered effort. Uplifting the bid-derived centre of ~1,500 PD by 25% gives ~1,875. The actuals-derived centre (A1, A2) is ~1,600. Reconciling these, and weighting the actuals-derived evidence more heavily, gives a stage-corrected median of **~1,700 PD**.

**The relative anchors then supply the shape, not the centre.** R4's cone gives a P10-P90 span of roughly ×0.6 to ×2.0 around the stage-corrected median at this stage. R1 places roughly one project in six at or above a doubling, which pins the region just below P90. R2 and R3 say the tail beyond that is power-law and is not thinned by this project being small. R7 says the observed tail is censored and therefore optimistic.

Note the internal consistency check that matters: R1's "one in six above 200%" and my P80 = 1.6× / P90 = 2.0× are compatible — R1 places the doubling point at about the 83rd percentile, my curve at the 90th. I sit slightly conservative relative to Flyvbjerg there, deliberately, because R1's sample is weighted toward larger and more governance-heavy projects than this class member.

**A calendar sanity check, independent of the effort arithmetic.** P50 = 1,700 PD against the A3 team (≈7 nominal FTE, ≈6.5 effective) at 220 charged days per person-year gives **12-14 months elapsed** to first production release. Anchor A5, built from calendar observations rather than effort, gives a class median of 10-14 months. These agree. They also both sit *above* the nine months that a supplier looking at this RFP would instinctively plan — which is precisely what the class predicts and what a bottom-up method, working from the parts, tends not to see.

**Currency.** All my absolute anchors are undated relative to the RFP. This RFP is dated 2016; the anchors are drawn from a body of practice spanning roughly 2000-2020. Whether the estimate is meant to be "what this would have cost in 2016" or "what this class costs now" changes the productivity assumption and I cannot resolve it from the outside view. See blind spot 5.

**Skew: strongly right. Deliberately not symmetrised.** P50 − P10 = 700 PD. P90 − P50 = 1,700 PD. The upper half of the P10-P90 interval is 2.4× the lower half, and beyond P90 the ratio widens further. Any presentation of this as "1,700 ± 800" is a misreading of the class.

### The scenario behind each quantile

**P10 — 1,000 PD.** *The class's lucky run.* The client's APIs are as documented and a counterpart answers within a day. The SSO is a standard protocol configured, not negotiated. The declared narrowings (A4, A5, A8, A9) all hold, and the client confirms them early rather than discovering them at UAT. The team stabilises in the first month and does not churn. The client's security and data-protection reviews are passed on first submission. UAT finds defects, not requirements. Nothing is re-platformed. This happens; it is roughly a one-in-ten class outcome; it is not the outcome to plan against.

**P50 — 1,700 PD.** *The class's ordinary run.* Roughly one integration turns out harder than assumed, in a way visible only after the counterparty's documentation is actually read. Enterprise approval cycles cost real weeks — A6 is right that this is visible only to the outside view. Two or three requirements are re-read during the build, none catastrophically. UAT produces a round of rework beyond what was planned. Environment and release management take longer than the estimate implied because a hosted hand-over has obligations that only surface when someone tries to hand over. The first release ships, later than planned, roughly whole.

**P80 — 2,700 PD.** *The class's ordinary bad run.* All of the P50 events, plus: one significant re-reading of a requirement that forces rework of something already built; a period of client unavailability that stalls decisions; team churn at a bad moment; a non-functional obligation (availability, disaster recovery, or a data-protection review) that turns out to demand architectural change rather than configuration. The project is late enough that its lateness becomes a topic in its own right, which consumes further effort in reporting and replanning.

**P90 — 3,400 PD.** *Top of the accumulation regime.* Roughly a doubling of the stage-corrected median, which the class literature places at about the 83rd-90th percentile. Several of the P80 mechanisms fire together and compound: rework on a subsystem that is itself still changing. The team has grown, and growth in this regime is partly self-defeating. This is the last point at which the project is still recognisably the project that was estimated — beyond here, the generating process changes.

**P95 — 4,600 PD.** Regime change on one axis. Scenario given in full at §2.4.

### Part 2 — project-specific notes from this run, kept separate

These are observations about this particular input, recorded so the diagnostician can attribute divergence. They are not part of the static list above and they did not move any quantile.

- **The obligation count was quarantined.** A1's "68 product obligations and 5 items of demanded work" is a decomposition artefact and I declined to use it. If another sensor's output correlates with 68 and mine does not, that is by design, not by disagreement.
- **The team was given, not inferred.** A3 fixed a parameter the outside view normally infers. It happened to agree with the class norm, so it neither widened nor narrowed my range — but a reader should know that one input to my team × duration anchor came from the log rather than from the class.

A1 is right that they need a term before they can be priced, and right that the instrument is a rate per unit time. My distribution would be read wrongly if it were taken to cover them.

- **A11 states there is no client behind this RFP.** In the real class, ten unasked questions would be asked and roughly half would come back with answers that narrow the range. Here they cannot be. My distribution is therefore the *unasked* distribution, which is wider than the one a real supplier would carry into a real bid after one clarification call.
